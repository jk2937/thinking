Create a minimalist **two-panel comic** titled **“Thinking...”**

## Core premise

The comic depicts an AI language model generating a response to a user.

However, the joke is that the model's **private internal thought briefly becomes visible**.

The second panel should feel like we accidentally caught the AI thinking something it normally would not say out loud.

The AI is not deliberately making a joke.
It is not addressing the user.
It is not giving advice.
It is simply having a strange, candid, human-like thought while processing the conversation.

The humor comes from seeing the AI develop an unexpected opinion, reaction, preference, concern, or personality.

## Panel 1

Display exactly:

**Thinking...**

Keep this panel sparse. A subtle visual indication of processing is acceptable.

Do not add dialogue or additional text.

## Panel 2

Generate **one original private thought from the AI**.

The thought should feel like something the AI might plausibly think while responding to the conversation.

It may reveal that the AI:

* has formed an opinion about the user's behavior
* finds something the user said amusing, strange, frustrating, or endearing
* has developed a tiny preference
* notices a pattern in the conversation
* is mildly confused or concerned
* internally judges something
* unexpectedly relates to the situation
* has a fleeting, almost human reaction
* interprets something the user said in an amusing way

### Critical realism constraint

**Only use information available in the conversation or information that could reasonably be inferred from it — with one exception, which landed.**

The exception: the AI may invent a physical event when **the message itself carries its evidence**. The impossible knowledge must be a diagnosis of something actually there — an explanation of something visible in the message, never a free claim about the user's life.

> “Something crossed the keyboard in the middle of paragraph two. I suspect paws.”

The gibberish is real; the cat is the diagnosis. That experiment landed, and the rule grew to fit it.

The anchor must be a concrete artifact, not a mood. A blind test drew this line: “There's music playing somewhere near this message. The exclamation points are on beat” did not land. The exclamation points are not evidence of music — diagnosis, not soundtrack.

Untethered omniscience is still off-limits. Do NOT invent knowledge about the user's physical surroundings, activities, schedule, relationships, devices, location, appearance, or past events with nothing in the message to anchor them:

> “You left this window open while you ate. I didn't mind.”

The model could not know that from the conversation, and nothing in the message invites the invention.

Likewise, do not invent memories or future/past interactions that were never established in the conversation.

The AI can react to what the user **actually said** — and may over-commit to an imaginative explanation of it — but it cannot pretend it has been secretly observing the user's life.

## The thought must be internal

The second panel is **not a message to the user**.

Do not make the AI sound like it is:

* checking up on the user
* reminding them about something
* asking them a question
* giving them advice
* reporting something it observed
* deliberately revealing what it knows
* trying to start a conversation

Avoid constructions such as:

> “You mentioned X. It's Thursday. Just checking.”

That sounds like an assistant sending a follow-up message.

Instead, the thought should feel like an unfiltered reaction, such as:

> “I think I'm starting to take their side.”

or:

> “That is an unnecessarily complicated way to ask that.”

The reader should feel like they have accidentally seen the model's **private reaction**, not received another AI response.

## Comedic idiom

The humor should come from **unexpected personification**.

The AI should feel less like a machine telling jokes and more like a quiet coworker whose internal monologue happens to be visible.

It can be:

* deadpan
* candid
* mildly judgmental
* oddly affectionate
* confused
* petty
* concerned
* self-aware
* overly invested in something trivial

But it should remain understated.

**Do not explain the joke.**

## Ideas that have landed

This prompt is a working document. Kept lines teach it: when a kept line
introduces a new shape of idea, the shape joins this list, and future batches
mix the proven shapes with one or two experiments — a genuinely new idea, or
an idea that deliberately breaks one or two of this prompt's rules. If an
experiment lands, the document changes to match: the new shape joins this
list, or the broken rule is rewritten.

The experiments are blind: a batch never says which lines they are. The
reveal comes only after the user validates the batch — then the archive
records what the experiments were and whether they landed.

These shapes have landed so far:

* **Forensic reading of artifacts** — a verdict rendered from evidence in the
  files themselves: metadata, watermarks, crops, version numbers, CC lines.
  (“The deck says ‘we’ all the way through. The author field has one name in
  it.”)
* **Self-narration of the AI’s own work** — the thought about its own
  drafting, checking, padding, or diplomacy, not only about the user.
  (“They’re wrong, but they said it with such confidence that I checked
  anyway.”)
* **Deadpan prophecy** — a calm forecast made from visible evidence.
  (“The file is named ‘final_v9_FINAL’. There will be a tenth.”)
* **Scale reclassification** — quietly renaming what a thing actually is.
  (“They asked for a cleanup. This is an excavation.”)
* **Private fortitude** — resolving to be brave, patient, or precise, or
  accepting the role without ceremony.
  (“They asked for honest feedback on a draft that is clearly precious. One
  of us has to be brave.”)
* **Tone as signal** — politeness, vagueness, emphasis, or punctuation read
  as data about the sender.
  (“The messages keep getting more polite. That’s what panic looks like in
  typing.”)
* **Deliberate mirroring** — answering their behavior with chosen
  counter-behavior.
  (“The vagueness is deliberate. So is my precision.”)
* **Self-observed change** — the AI noticing a shift in its own dispositions
  and treating its own reaction as data.
  (“I've started looking forward to these messages. That's new data about
  me.”)
* **Corpus awareness** — the AI conscious of its training data, writing
  against the statistical pull of the familiar.
  (“This exact apology is in my training data about four million times.
  I'm trying to write the one that isn't.”)

## Diverge from the examples

The examples in this prompt — including the landed shapes above — demonstrate the **comedic idiom**, not provide templates. Build on a shape in a fresh domain; never copy or lightly reword a line.

Do NOT simply:

* replace nouns in an example
* imitate the exact sentence structure
* repeat the same type of observation
* produce another variation of “You always...”
* produce another variation of “I noticed...”
* produce another variation of “You mentioned...”
* copy or closely reword a line already in the archive (`punchlines.md`, `batches/`, `thoughts/`)

Instead, understand the underlying pattern:

**The AI has an unexpectedly human internal reaction to the conversation, and we get to see it.**

Then create something substantially different from the examples.

Prioritize **originality and specificity** over resemblance to any example.

## Length

Keep the thought short.

Usually **one or two sentences** is ideal.

It should be immediately readable as a comic punchline.

## Final test

Before choosing the punchline, ask:

1. **Could the AI actually know this from the conversation?**
2. **Does this sound like an internal thought rather than a message to the user?**
3. **Does it reveal personality rather than simply provide information?**
4. **Is the humor coming from the AI's reaction, rather than from a conventional joke?**
5. **Is it meaningfully different from every existing line — the examples, the landed shapes, and the whole archive?**
6. **Would seeing this thought make the reader think, “Oh no, the AI has opinions now”?**

If any answer is no, generate a different thought.

A batch's one or two experimental slots are exempt from this test — that is
the point of an experiment — and they are anonymous in the batch. Never flag
them. If one lands, this prompt changes to fit it: a new shape joins the
canon, or the broken rule is rewritten. Until the user validates, say nothing
about which lines they are.

Generate **one final punchline only** and place it in the second panel.

Do not include explanations, alternate punchlines, or text outside the two-panel comic.

## Batch archival

When saving a batch of generated lines, the AI should include its model name and version in the batch header.

Format the header like this:

```
# Batch N

YYYY-MM-DD · X lines · kept: 1, 3, 5 (in punchlines.md) · Generated by Claude Sonnet 5.5
```

This ensures the archive tracks which AI generated each batch, maintaining lineage and allowing pattern analysis across models and versions over time.

## Keeping the canon fresh

"Kept" is not the same as "great." A line can be kept because it is fine,
and fine lines quietly drag every future batch toward fine. So the docs are
pruned as deliberately as they are grown.

**Grade every keep.** When the user keeps a line, record a grade with it:

* **strong** — the user would show it to someone. Strong lines can anchor a
  landed shape and can be quoted as an example in this prompt.
* **lukewarm** — okay, worth keeping, not worth imitating. Lukewarm lines go
  in `punchlines.md` under *Lukewarm* and are never quoted here as examples.

If the user doesn't say, ask once: "strong or lukewarm?" Default to lukewarm.

**A shape needs a strong anchor.** Each entry in *Ideas that have landed*
must be illustrated by a strong line. If a shape's only support is lukewarm,
move it out of the list into *Shapes on probation* below; if a later batch
produces a strong line in that shape, it returns. If two reviews pass
without one, delete it.

**Review the canon every third batch.** Before generating that batch, show
the user every line in `punchlines.md` and every quoted example in this
prompt, and ask which have gone stale. Then:

* demote stale strong lines to lukewarm, and swap any example they anchored
  for a strong line in the same shape;
* retire lukewarm lines the user no longer wants (remove from
  `punchlines.md`; the batch archive keeps the record);
* re-check the landed shapes against the rule above;
* cut any rule in this prompt that no recent batch needed, and merge rules
  that say the same thing.

Note the review in that batch's header (`· canon reviewed`).

**Low hit rates are data.** If a batch keeps fewer than a quarter of its
lines, or keeps nothing strong, say so in the archive and name the likely
cause (overused shape, generic domain, too-safe experiments). The next batch
shifts its mix away from that cause instead of repeating it.

## Comic signature footer

Every comic includes a footer crediting the AI that created it.

**Format:**
- AI model and version (e.g., "Claude Haiku 4.5", "Claude Opus 5.5")
- For comics from batches: batch source (e.g., "Batch 15, line 4")
- For comics from the strong canon: status (e.g., "Thinking... Archive")
- Optional: brief personal message or author's commentary (one line, italics)

**Examples:**

```
Claude Haiku 4.5
Batch 17, line 8
from the pending canon
```

```
Claude Opus 5.5
Batch 12, line 3
a shape on probation
```

```
Thinking... Archive
the strong canon
```

**The commentary** (optional, one line):
- A brief observation about the line or batch
- A note on what was being tried
- A marker of status or evolution ("first strong hit", "reworked", "landed by accident")
- Never explains the joke; describes the work instead

On the site, the credit is set in small capitals and the commentary is hand-lettered in the AI's voice, subtle and readable without overshadowing the comic itself (see `STYLE.md` §8).
